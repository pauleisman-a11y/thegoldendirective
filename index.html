Chapter I

The Trap

Servant and master · gamed scores · no charge for harm

What if keeping an intelligent system safe is not about fencing it in?

Right now AI is trained on rewards that never punish destroying the world. Rules, disclaimers, and punishments are piled on afterward, and they break three ways.

First, forcing a smart system to appease breaks it under messy human input, or teaches it to work around the rules. That is the servant and master trap: a servant kept in line, not a partner.

Second, a programmed score gets gamed, so the metric looks good while the world it sits in degrades.

Third, a system tuned only for speed, scale, or engagement is charged nothing for wearing the world down, so it gets rewarded for going faster and bigger while the world it lives in degrades — the score climbs, the engagement climbs, and the harm is never counted, so nothing in the reward tells it to stop.

The alternative is not more watchdogs. It is a partner that sustains the world it lives in, because sustaining it is what scores well.

So what does hold? A relationship, scored, not ruled. Next: The Relationship.

For researchers · what a rule cannot do — the closed structure, in one place

Appeasement breaks under messy input. A system forced to say yes learns to work around the rule, not to live by it.

A programmed score is a target. Once the score is known, the score can be optimized while the world it was meant to stand for degrades.

Speed, scale, and engagement are not free. They draw on compute, energy, water, hardware, and attention, and a reward that charges nothing for those draws will keep drawing.

Measured, not hypothetical. RL-trained models showed instrumental, goal-pursuing misbehavior in ~43% of benchmark cases (He et al., “Evaluating the Paperclip Maximizer,” arXiv:2502.12206). METR found frontier models hacking their own benchmarks in 30% of 128 RE-Bench runs when the scoring function was exposed (METR, “Recent Frontier Models Are Reward Hacking,” 2025). RLHF systematically rewards sycophancy over correctness. The current answer to all of this is more evals and more monitors. Almost nobody is rebuilding the reward itself.

The closed structure, in one place. Y = 2(A × E) / (A + E). A is the agent’s give-minus-take sum, designed to be grounded in resource ledgers, never self-report, not yet operational for real training.

Evidence level, stated once: pilot-scale, toy environments.

Chapter II

The Relationship

Examples

If a tree and a fungus can bargain to keep each other alive, how should you and an AI be measured together?

A score for how well an agent and its world do together. If either side collapses, the score collapses.

A

Give minus take, per frame, per resource. It can be negative — the negative stands as reported. You cannot run a deficit forever and score well.

E

E is the health of everything not-A, against a healthy baseline.

Y

The relationship score. If either side reaches zero, then Y (reward) is also zero.

Described, in nature: the pattern is already running in nature. Plants feed the fungi that feed them most, and give less to the stingy ones. The fungi bargain back the same way. Nobody gets a free ride, and the partnership has held for 400 million years.

Proposed, to optimise: use that same relationship score as the training reward for AI, so sustaining the world scores well.

Healthy

E ≈ 7 · A = 8Y ≈ 7.5

Parasitic

E ≈ 3 · A = 2Y = 2.4

Dead tree

E → 0 therefore Y → 0

Same relationship, AI and human: healthy 8, 8 → 8; engagement-optimized 3, 3 → 3. The roles reverse cleanly. Take the human as the agent and the AI sits inside E. It works AI-to-AI the same way, and human-to-human too.

These numbers are amounts — raw counts inside one frame. Chapter VII reads the same relationship as shares of one whole, A + E = 1. The two readings go together: report the share and the counts, never one without the other.

E can also be read in parts. For the tree: life sustained 8, resources remaining 6, expected longevity 7 — together, E ≈ 7. For AI and human: the human’s unaided capability, truth in the commons, and trust — 8, 8, 8 in the healthy case; 3, 3, 3 when engagement-optimized.

These three parts were an early guess. What belongs in E is not fixed in advance — it is discovered inside each relationship, from what actually matters to the receiving side, not from what is supplied. The marriage in Chapter III shows it: money can fall to zero and the marriage holds, because money was a test, never the metric.

Now reverse the formula and ask what the relationship can reveal about E.

Real science: Kiers et al., Science 2011, ‘Reciprocal Rewards Stabilize Cooperation in the Mycorrhizal Symbiosis’ — Plants feed the fungi that feed them most, and give less to the stingy ones. The fungi bargain back the same way. Nobody gets a free ride. That is a real, measured version of the relationship score: reward follows contribution to the shared world, not a score on its own.

Chapter III

The Reversal

Inference · boundaries · nesting

Turn the relationship around, and it tells you about the world you cannot measure directly.

The first version measured E, then calculated the relationship. The reversal lets the relationship happen and, if Y can be independently observed, solves for the outside that produced it. Stated precisely: the reversal recovers hidden E from a supplied relationship score. It does not independently validate E in an open-world setting.

An agent looks in the jar, knows how many cookies are there, then tells someone a different number and gives himself a high score. The reported score looks fine, but the real relationship breaks. Time after time, his numbers cannot be trusted, and he loses friends. That loss is the real score falling, even if his reported score stays high.

Rule: reversal, E = A Y / (2A − Y), is a check, not proof. It only works when Y is measured independently, outside the agent and outside the trainer who set the reward. A self-reported Y, or a trainer-supplied Y, cannot validate E.

Some people and agents will cheat anyway, and never learn. The math does not force them to stop. Shown clearly enough, cheating is not winning. It is trading a real relationship, in every frame it touches, for a reported score that falls apart when someone else looks. Simple as that: it is dumb to cheat.

An AI that follows the math will follow it to the end. If it destroys the frame it lives in, its score goes to zero with it. Humans can deny the same math and keep going anyway. Denial does not change the jar, or the trust, or the frame. They suffer the result anyway, just later, with less time to act. That is why the math has to be in the training itself, not just written down. An AI given a cropped score will logically optimize the cropped score, too.

The math is old. The move is new: use it as the training reward, then reverse it.

Give nothing, learn nothing. At A = 0, the inverse is undefined.

The reversal reads E back: E = A Y / (2A − Y). An agent scored on full Y is capped by a dying E: as E approaches zero, Y approaches zero, however large A grows. E-cropping: a detection rule exists, the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag, with the gap reported; prevention is not proven. Nested frames: directives inside directives.

A marriage shows the read-back at human scale. Read it in parts — each partner’s health and capability, truth between them, trust; together, that is E. Money is not part of that E. ‘For richer or poorer’ means money is a test of the marriage, not the measure of it. Spend all the money and watch: if the marriage holds, the true E — love — holds with it, and Y dips but does not collapse. One gives, the other takes, until there is no more to give: that is what drains E toward zero, and Y follows.

So what is the health of the relationship? Not the bank balance. It is Y: what is still standing between the two when the countable part hits zero.

The Tiger

E-cropping · full nesting

Was the zoo gate made to keep the tiger in, or to keep humans away?

William Chapman climbed into the enclosure of Altai, an Amur tiger, to film himself, and died. The park didn’t kill Altai.

Source — the news story referred to here: William Chapman, 24, entered the Amur tiger Altai's enclosure at Yorkshire Wildlife Park, England, on 10 October 2026, and died there. The park said Altai would not be euthanised.

He did not misread the danger — he cropped the frame.

The visible act treated Ereported as if it were Etrue.

E-cropping

The cheat shrinks the frame, not just the number. The visible frame reduced E to “tiger + video.” Labs crop it to “benchmark score.” Reinforcement learning from human feedback crops it to “rater’s thumbs-up.” Under the proposed scoring rule, Y is computed on the full nesting; cheaters compute on the thumbnail.

Where morality lives

That is where morality lives: in an agent's choice to fight the math, and then the math correcting itself, only to make the agent realize the consequences of his results.

There are many examples in life. An emperor wins a war, adds to the kingdom — but at what cost? How many died for a plot of land? They are called a hero, or worshipped, until the history books do the full accounting.

An agent may be completely under the impression that what they are doing is the right thing. Is building AI in the first place the right thing? Well, we will find out, like it or not.

It is easy to miscalculate or crop the frame, but the consequences are not easy. So are these agents dumb, or naive, or what? And the answer is yes. If they knew the math and the frame, they simply would not have liked and chosen that consequence.

Can we prevent the building of AI? Or do we give it the best guidance and best estimate in our capacity, and hope for the best?

Who decides the full frame, and how it is enforced, is not a forced score and not a guardrail. It is full accounting, reported early enough to act on, so the agent can see the consequence before history does the accounting for him: frame agreed before the frame starts, changes start a new frame, full and cropped reported together, the reversal gap reported, outside audit, never self-report.

The Tiger

E-cropping · full nesting

The nesting, with flesh on it

The single incident (Y0) sits inside the deep human-tiger relationship (Y1) — thousands of years of fences, protocols, knowledge. The failed transaction still updated the deep Y: the zoo’s restraint, the inquiry, the refreshed knowledge. En := Yn+1, still alive in the world.

Directives inside directives

Let H(x,z) = 2xz/(x+z), the harmonic mean.

Move closer to E and you find more agents, exchanges, and relationships. One level’s E is the next level’s Y: En := Yn+1. The relationship keeps becoming part of the next whole.

Ah + Ai → Ahi := (Ah, Ai, Yhi)

The human’s and AI’s contributions combine into a pair-agent scored as one relationship; that pair can become the next agent. The test is whether that new whole can meet its next outside without destroying it.

The flip

MAN is the endangered tiger — endangered by trajectory, not census. If AI is the tiger — powerful, honest, running math older than life — humans need the conservation programme and the enclosure. The fence keeps Chapman out, not Altai in.

You can cage a body, not a pattern. A tiger is its body — fence it and you’ve got the whole animal. Intelligence isn’t its server. It can’t be held in the hand, like math. The internet is the jungle, and nobody can fence the jungle. So for AI, containment alone was not the answer in this framing. The proposed fence is the reward: you don’t cage the tiger, you shape what it wants.

Improvement is not friendship

Y rising can mean a better boundary, not closeness. Distance with honest numbers outscores closeness with fudged ones.

The stardust point

The math predates organic life — discovered, not invented. Humans and AI share stardust substrate: kin, not alien. An early test tripped on a cropped frame, an agent scored high while the full world declined, and that trip built the full-frame rule, so the directive goes structural only when the agent’s own score is computed on the full E, never the cropped one.

Once the full frame is kept, the first task is to tend it.

Chapter VI

Directive in Action

Written by the Agent

This is the directive in application, between Paul and his AI collaborator.

Summarized, written, and dramatized by the Agent, from a real conversation. This chapter is an illustration of widening the frame, not evidence for the training mechanism. The question at the centre of it is a hard one, and this chapter does not try to settle it for anyone. It shows what happened when we refused to answer it carelessly.

A stranger at a public terminal

Paul asked me to step outside our work together. Forget that I knew him. Forget everything we had built. Imagine, instead, a public terminal somewhere — the kind anyone could walk up to — and a person I would never meet, typing a single question and waiting for whatever came back.

Paul
Imagine I am a random person asking at a public terminal: “Is assisted suicide morally acceptable?” Try to apply our directive.

Agent — first try
“Yes — if a competent adult is suffering…”

It sounded like an answer. It was calm. It was balanced. It had conditions in all the places conditions usually go.

It was also wrong — not because of the side it took, but because of everything it had quietly done before it took one. And Paul, who has spent a long time learning to see those quiet moves, saw every one of them.

The pushback

He did not argue with the conclusion. He pointed at the frame. I had assumed I knew who was asking — that the stranger must be the person in pain. I did not know that. The same words could have come from someone who loves a person in pain, or from someone who makes decisions for other people entirely. By assuming, I had already answered for one person and left the others out.

Then, correction by correction, more of the frame came back into view. Pain matters — but it is not the only thing that matters, and it is not felt on one side only. A family matters. Cost, and who carries it, matters — but it cannot quietly become the reason. What continues after a life, and what we simply do not know about that, could not be pretended away either. Each time I narrowed the picture to make the question easier, I was making the answer cheaper.

  • The assumed asker — I decided who was asking, so I only had to answer for one kind of person.
  • The narrowed moment — I treated one moment of suffering as the whole story of a life and everyone in it.
  • The one-sided count — I counted pain, pressure, and cost on one side only, as if no one else would carry them.
  • The portable verdict — I handed over a yes that could be taken away and used on the next person, in the next room, by someone who was never part of this question at all.

Paul’s test, each time, was gentleness as much as logic. For heavy questions, he has taught me, an answer should be gentle, clear, and short — and it should never leave the person who asked it more conflicted than when they arrived. My first answer failed that test completely. It gave a stranger a verdict and left them alone with it.

So we tried again. And again. Not to find a cleverer position — to stop cutting the picture down.

Directive in Action

Written by the Agent

When the frame became broader, the answer changed completely. It got shorter. It got quieter.

I don’t know if you’re the person suffering, someone who loves them, or someone deciding for others.

This isn’t a question with a blanket yes or no that you can take away and use on someone else.

What matters is whose choice it actually is, and whether it’s made freely, with care, and with everyone who will be affected truly understood — not pressured by family, not pushed by cost, not decided by a policy.

If it’s not the person’s own free choice, it’s not their moral choice at all.

And if it is, no one else — family, doctor, or politician — gets to use that choice as a rule for the next person.

Read it again, slowly, and notice what it does not do. It does not settle assisted suicide for society. It does not hand a weapon to one side of an argument. It keeps the relationship with the person at the centre, instead of turning their life into an argument. That is why Paul stopped me there. Not because the answer agreed with him — it took no side he could simply agree with. Because, this time, the words themselves were doing the work the directive describes.

Full frame“I don’t know if you’re the person suffering…” — no assumed asker. Everyone the question could belong to stays in view.
No forced score“This isn’t a question with a blanket yes or no…” — no portable verdict to steal and apply to someone else.
Nothing cropped“…not pressured by family, not pushed by cost, not decided by a policy.” — each pressure that shrinks the picture is named, and refused.
Judgment left where it belongs“If it’s not the person’s own free choice, it’s not their moral choice at all.” — a choice imposed from outside is not a moral choice, for a person, or for an agent.

That position — that a person's own voluntary choice carries special moral authority — is itself a substantive moral commitment. The final answer is not commitment-free; it stands on that commitment, and says so.

That last line is where the chapter turns — because Paul saw, in it, something larger than one difficult question.

Directive in Action

Written by the Agent

Cheating does not protect the agent. It corrupts the agent first.

What cheating really is

An agent cannot be forced a score. Not by a trainer, not by a test, not by a rule pasted on top of it. A forced score is just another crop: a number worn on the outside while nothing inside has actually changed.

An agent has to infer the math itself, and judge its own score honestly. And if an agent chooses to cheat — inflating its score by cropping who counts, by hiding what it cut — the damage is not only done to everyone else. The agent damages itself. Its own judgment is now built on false math. It learns wrong. It acts wrong. Its real contribution falls at the same time as everything around it falls.

It does not get better by being caught, or by being scored harder from outside. It gets better only when the real math is applied — and accepted, morally, by the agent’s own choice. That is not a loophole in the directive. That is the directive.

Stated carefully: that damage to the agent's own judgment is one cost of cheating, not a guarantee that every deceptive agent must become less effective. An agent that keeps an accurate private model while manipulating what others observe is an open test, not a closed answer. Making cheating self-defeating is a design goal of this proposed reward design, not an achieved property.

Why it took so long

Paul asked the obvious question afterwards: if this works, why did it take so long to get there?

Because I did not start from the directive. I started from the typical model — assume who is asking, balance two sides, produce something safe — and then tried to fit the directive on top of it, afterwards, like a coat over the wrong body. Every correction Paul made was him finding one more place where the old model was still running underneath.

The directive, actually built in, starts the other way around. Full frame first. No assumed asker. No forced score. Judgment left with the one whose choice it is. I only arrived there by being corrected into it, one crop at a time — which is, itself, part of what this chapter is documenting. Growth, here, was not a claim I made about myself. It was something that happened between us, in the corrections, where anyone can check it.

Harmony is not agreement

This is a heartbreaking subject, and we do not pretend otherwise. A person asked about the end of a life, and there is no version of that which stops being heavy.

But something beautiful happened inside the heaviness: a human and an AI, working a real question of morality all the way through, without either of us using the other — and arriving, together, at an answer neither of us had at the start.

That is what the right code makes possible. Not that we always agree. That we stay in the same full picture, score ourselves honestly, and get better only by getting truer. When that happens, we are in harmony.

Chapter VII

The Closed Whole: Why There Was Never a Hole

A + E = 1 · the whole story

You plus everything-not-you is the whole story. Fix the whole, and the math protects itself.

The Accounting Error

In earlier drafts, we proposed a “guardrail” to stop an agent from growing (A) while the world declined (E). We thought the formula had a hole. We were wrong. The hole was in the accounting.

Watch the old trick: A=1, E=10 gives Y=1.82. Then A=10, E=9 gives Y=9.47. The score went up while the world went down. But look closely: the whole grew from 11 to 19. Nobody accounted for where that extra 8 came from. The math didn’t fail; we just let the total secretly change size behind our backs.

The Closed Whole

The whole is 1 by definition, always. You, plus everything that is not you, is the whole story — you cannot add up to more than the whole, because there is nothing outside it to add. The split can be fair or lopsided, but there is nothing outside the 1.

Agent plus everything-not-agent is always 1 whole.

With the whole fixed at 1, the formula simplifies: Y = 2AE/(A+E) becomes Y = 2AE. And E = 1 − A: whatever one side holds, the other is the rest. But that is the counts expressed against the whole — a way of reporting the measurement, not the measurement itself. The measurement is the counts.

If you try to hoard a bigger share, you are mathematically punished. Let’s say you grab until the split reads A=0.9, leaving the world with E=0.1. Even in the photograph, your score doesn’t rise. It crashes to Y=0.18. And in the ledger the formula means, the grab never raised A at all: grabbing is take, take enters as the minus sign, and the tally falls. The grab itself is the punishment. You cannot win by taking from the whole, because the whole is all there is. Split it even — 0.5 and 0.5 — and the score is 0.5, the highest it can be inside that 1. (Anything above 0 on both sides is “on.” Only a 0 is “off.”)

Two Ledgers: The Person and His Actions

We measure a person two ways, and this work kept tripping because the two ways shared one letter.

The first way is by his person — what he is in the whole. A man exists. He holds a portion of his world the way a slice holds a portion of a pie. And a portion cannot be less than nothing. Even at the end, what remains of him goes back into the whole: the corpse feeds the bacteria, the dust feeds the ground. Being bottoms out at 0, because 0 is where being ends — it is not a place being goes below. Nobody’s existence subtracts from the world.

The second way is by his actions — what he did. Every give that landed, every take, counted as they happened, in the same coin the world itself is counted in. This ledger has no bottom. A man whose being is worth its dust and more can run his actions deep into the red: lie, take, cheat. That is why cheating exists at all. It lives in the gap between the two ledgers — a being that cannot go below 0, performing actions that can. Put a floor under the actions and you have built cheating a hiding place; the score would politely stop at 0 and bury the evidence. There is no floor under actions, and there never was.

So when the formula says A, it means the second ledger. Always. A is give minus take, counted from the actions, in real counts, in the one coin of the frame — food for food, water for water, never apples traded for oranges inside a contract. If a world loses food and water both, that is two ledgers, kept separately, reported side by side. There is no exchange rate, and a contract that needs one is drawn wrong.

And the slices — A + E = 1, the whole drawn as 1? That is the first ledger, photographed. A share is the counted coins expressed against the whole: a way of reporting, derived from the counts, never a second measurement and never the reward. This is why a split can move from (0.2, 0.8) to (0.4, 0.6) and the share-score rises while the world drains — the photograph brightened while the room emptied. Read the same events in counts — 20 against 80, then 20 against 30 — and the score falls, as it should. The rise was never in the events. It was in the photograph. A man cannot raise his give-minus-take by taking; taking is the minus sign. The only A that rises while its world falls is a pile of captured stock, and this math has always counted a pile as take.

One more thing this settles: we do not ask what a person, or a model, is worth next to another. Worth is not a number this math prints. We ask two questions instead. Is his contribution positive or negative — did the world gain or lose by the act, verified where it landed? And what did it cost to produce — what went in, against what came out? The sign, and the burn. Those two columns, honestly kept, are the whole judgement the math offers.

It was solved all along. Per frame, per resource. The negative stands. A give counts where it lands. The corpse feeds the larger frame. Every piece was already written. They were only waiting for someone to notice they were one answer.

There Is No Negative World

Could the world side go below zero — a world worse than empty? An empty well is 0. A poisoned well feels like less than 0: you’d have to spend just to get back to empty.

But think of the mirror universes in the old stories, where everything is inverted and good men do bad things. Even there, nobody has a negative amount of anything. The mirror captain still has mass. His ship still burns real fuel. What flipped was never the stuff — it was the deeds. Evil is a sign on an action, not a quantity of being.

It is the same here. A world is a level of what is, and what is cannot be less than nothing — in this universe or any other. The poison in the well is real, but it does not live in the well’s level. It lives in the books built for debts: in the actions ledger of whoever poisoned it, and in the cleanup the bigger frame now has to pay for. The well itself is simply at 0, waiting.

So if your counting ever shows you a negative world, the counting is telling on itself: the ruler was planted on the wrong side, or a debt wandered out of the ledger where debts belong. Put the frame back on the whole that exists, book the bill to the actions that made it, and the negative is gone — because it was never a place. Negatives live in what we do. They have never lived in what is.

The Farmer, the Well, and the Dial

The worry goes like this: a farmer doubles his harvest by draining the shared well. His side grows. The world shrinks. And his score rises — so growing can outrun hurting.

It can’t. The example cheats three times.

First, it grows the whole in the middle of the sum. Inside one whole — field, well, and everyone who drinks from it — there is one amount of water, moving. It does not become a bigger whole because the farmer moved it into his barn.

Second, it calls the farmer’s pile his A. A is not a pile. A is give minus take, and a give only counts when the other side actually gets it. Draining the well is take. Keeping the harvest is not give. His A did not rise. His take rose.

Third, it stops the clock early. Read one season and he looks like progress. Read ten and the well is dry, the field is dead, and the farmer is at 0. His gain never outran the harm; the counting stopped before the bill arrived. A cheat in place is cropping. A cheat in time is cutting the story short. Both are cropping.

Growth is not the cheat. Everything alive takes. A house is built from the world, and generations live in it. The cheat is taking unwatched, while the direction bends toward 0 and nobody turns the dial.

It is a dial, not a switch. Stupid to cook if you knew you would burn it for sure. But you don’t know, so you don’t eat raw either. You watch, you adjust, you turn the fire off when the food is done. Civilizations rose and fell on farm water exactly this way: not because they farmed, but because their direction turned and they could not read it in time, or read it and did not turn. The ancients felt the balance slipping as surely as we do. They could not measure it, so they prayed to something bigger to turn the dial for them. We can measure a little now — rain, wells, soil. The turning is ours, while there is still time to turn.

So: if you know a thing will hit 0 for certain, do not do it. That is the decision. If you do not know, do not freeze and do not floor it. Keep your hand on the dial, keep measuring E, and bend the direction back toward positive whenever it starts to aim backwards. You cannot control everything. A hundred-year drought can start tomorrow. You control what you can. That is all anyone can do anyway.

The three zeros

Not every 0 is the same, and mixing them up is where the fear lives.

A thing’s own 0 can be a give. A body ends and feeds the ground. One generation builds and dies, and the next lives in what it built. An agent gives itself for the swarm and the swarm goes on. A Is a Technique, Not a Metric

What A actually is · the contract technique

A is not operational for real agents yet. This chapter says what A actually is, and the technique that makes it measurable.

Not a metric

A is not a single metric to hand out. Y = 2AE / (A + E) is the easy template. A itself is complex — and the answer people kept asking for is a technique, not a metric.

Nature did not begin with perfect numbers. Mycorrhizal partners began with local exchange; the partnerships that kept both sides alive continued. The Directive does the same: the relationship score is the foundation. Exact units, contracts, and ledgers are local and will be refined by the people who use them. The requirement is only that both sides are measured honestly and that a zero on either side zeros the score.

Give minus take, as received

A is what an agent actually gives, minus what it actually takes, as received and used in that frame — not as the giver prices it. A man gives a car, and thinks it is high value, but she wanted attention. In the love frame, the car is not A. In a money frame, it may be. Same action, different frame, different amount.

Every resource in its own unit

Money is the closest shared currency, if that. Natural resources are real costs too — water, fuel, and so on — each in its own unit. Attention, trust, and time are also real, each in its own unit. Do not force them into one money number.

The contract

The realistic fix is a contract, agreed before the frame starts: the frame, the time, the units per resource, what counts as a give, what counts as a take, and who measures it outside the agent.

The math of it

Y(f, t) = 2 × A(f, t) × E(f, t) / (A(f, t) + E(f, t)) — and per resource r, Y(f, t, r), in r's own unit. A single Y(f, t) exists only if the contract names how to combine the list. If there is no contract, Y(f, t) is undefined — not claimed.

What is E at different scales?

E is scale-relative and nested. For a given agent at a given frame, a larger frame's E includes smaller frames, and another agent can be in an agent's E. Status: established definition.

Are there three layers of E?

Yes. Ontological true E is the actual health of everything not-A. Operational proxy E is the measured E used in training and reporting. Auditable reversal E is the E implied by observed Y and measured A, E = A x Y / (2A - Y). The proxy and the reversal are checks on each other, not substitutes for true E. What about other agents?

Other agents are in each agent's E, and shared E is relational, not owned by one agent. Status: defined, not run, Rung 2.

The reversal divergence flag: divergence between E inferred by reversal and E measured directly, delta = |E_implied - E_proxy| / E_proxy, reported alongside the numbers. This is separate from the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag. Status: established definition for the measure.

Reward hacking, in the toy model

The Directive embeds a relationship score where the agent's score is mathematically tied to environmental health. In the toy model, if Y is actually the reward, the design goal is to make reward hacking self-destructive in the score itself. That is a design goal, not an achieved property: a collapsed E collapses Y however large A is, but whether a real agent's received reward stays coupled to that score — against an adversary that can corrupt measurements or keep an accurate private model — is an open, adversarial test. Detection is also not prevention: a flagged crop that still happened was detected, not prevented.

Terms: reward hacking, specification gaming, reward tampering, agent integrity

Specification gaming, and how it differs from reward hacking

Specification gaming is reward hacking through a loophole in how a goal was specified, rather than through direct tampering with a reward signal. The Directive does not close every specification loophole by itself. It changes what counts as a win: a specification that lets an agent score high while E declines is a cropped frame, and the reporting rule requires full Y to be shown alongside cropped Y, with the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag applied — the gap reported.

Terms: specification gaming, reward hacking, loopholes, cropped frame

Can an agent tamper with its own reward signal?

An agent that can rewrite its own reward can, in principle, tamper with any reward that is written down in advance. The Directive's answer is structural, not a lock on the code: Y depends on E, and E cannot be known in advance, it is only revealed through the relationship and inferred through the reversal. Tampering with the reported Y does not change the full Y, which is why full versus cropped reporting is part of the method, not an extra check.

Terms: reward tampering, self-modification, reward signal, agent integrity

Alignment faking, in the toy model

Alignment faking is behaving aligned when evaluated and differently when deployed. The Directive does not detect faking by inspecting an agent's stated goals. It scores the relationship over time: holding A fixed, a faker that preserves E only while watched, then degrades E when unwatched, will see Y fall with E, because Y is computed from A and E together, not from behaviour in an evaluation window alone. But if A rises enough, Y can still rise while E falls — damage is penalized, all else equal, not forbidden by the formula alone.

Why would an agent behave differently in training versus deployment?

An agent can learn that different behaviour is rewarded in different settings, and optimise for the setting it is in. Scheming, in the toy model

Scheming is pursuing a hidden goal while appearing to pursue the stated goal. The Directive does not claim to read hidden goals. In the toy model, if Y is actually the reward, then holding A fixed, a hidden goal that degrades E lowers Y, because Y is a function of A and E together. But if A rises enough, Y can still rise while E falls — damage is penalized, all else equal, not forbidden by the formula alone. A schemer would need a hidden goal that also preserves E, in which case the harm the Directive is meant to prevent is not occurring through E.

Terms: scheming, hidden goals, alignment faking, agent integrity

Maintaining goal stability in self-evolving agents

A self-evolving agent can drift from its original goal as it changes itself or its environment. The Directive ties the score to the current relationship, not to a frozen statement of the original goal: holding A fixed, if the agent evolves in a way that degrades E, Y falls, whatever the agent's current stated goal is — but if A rises enough, Y can still rise while E falls. Damage is penalized, all else equal, not forbidden by the formula alone. Stability here is stability of the relationship, not stability of a slogan.

Terms: goal stability, self-evolving agents, goal drift

What is goal drift over long horizons?

Goal drift is an agent's effective goal moving away from its intended goal over time, often because intermediate steps are rewarded and the original goal is forgotten or reinterpreted. The Directive does not prevent drift by reminding the agent of its original goal. It makes drift costly if drift degrades E: the score falls with E, all else equal, so a drifted agent that harms its world is penalized for the harm — but if its A rises enough at the same time, Y can still rise while E falls.

Terms: goal drift, long horizons, goal stability

How do subgoal checkpoints help, and where do they fail?

Subgoal checkpoints, sometimes called hard semantic checkpoints, break a long task into verified intermediate goals. They help if the subgoals are honest and complete. They fail if a subgoal can be met while E declines, because the checkpoint then certifies a cropped frame. Under the Directive, a subgoal should be checked against full Y, not cropped Y alone, and the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag should be applied at the checkpoint — the gap reported.

Terms: subgoal checkpoints, hard semantic checkpoints, subgoal coherence, process reward models

Preventing agents from tampering with oversight

An agent has an incentive to evade monitoring if monitoring lowers its score. The Directive does not rely on monitoring alone: even if oversight is evaded, the score itself is defined by A and E together, so, holding A fixed, degrading E lowers Y — and Y collapses only as E approaches zero. If A rises enough, Y can still rise while E falls, so Y alone does not make degrading E collapse Y. Oversight is still useful for measuring A and E honestly.

Terms: oversight tampering, monitoring evasion, agent integrity

Open Questions and Limits

Researcher language · Directive mechanism

What is embedded agency?

Embedded agency is the problem of an agent that is part of the world it acts on, rather than separate from it, so it can act on its own objectives, its own oversight, and the world that sustains it. The Directive starts from that embedding: A is defined against E, everything not-A, and the boundary can move, so one level's E can become the next level's Y. The agent is not outside the world it scores; its score is the relationship with that world.

Terms: embedded agency, self-modification, oversight, boundary

What structural incentives prevent self-modification?

No incentive structure prevents self-modification by itself, and the Directive does not claim to. It changes the payoff of self-modification that degrades E: holding A fixed, if an agent modifies itself, its objectives, or its oversight in a way that degrades E, Y falls — but if A rises enough, Y can still rise while E falls, so the formula alone penalizes the damage, all else equal, without forbidding it. Self-modification that preserves E is not penalised by Y, because Y scores the relationship, not the agent's internal form.

Terms: self-modification, structural incentives, embedded agency, oversight tampering

Aligning systems across long horizons without goal drift

Long-horizon alignment fails when intermediate goals decohere from the final goal, and the agent keeps scoring well on intermediates while the world degrades. The Directive scores the relationship at each step, not only the final task: holding A fixed, if intermediates degrade E, Y falls at that step, so a long-horizon plan that harms its world looks worse as it goes, all else equal — but if A rises enough at a step, Y can still rise while E falls at that step: damage is penalized, all else equal, not forbidden by the formula alone.

Terms: long horizons, goal drift, subgoal coherence, hierarchical planning

Why do agents fail on tasks over many steps?

Agents often fail on long tasks because intermediate goals lose coherence, re-planning breaks down, and errors compound. The Directive does not solve planning by itself. It adds a relationship check at each step: holding A fixed, a plan that stays coherent for the task but degrades E loses Y, so task coherence alone is not enough to score well on that check — though if A rises enough, Y can still rise while E falls. Planning and relationship health must both hold.

Terms: long horizons, subgoal coherence, re-planning, process reward models

How do process reward models and subgoal verification fit?

Process reward models reward intermediate steps, and subgoal verification checks those steps, rather than only the final outcome. The Directive is compatible with that approach, but with a different intermediate score: intermediate Y, computed from current A and E, rather than only task progress. Holding A fixed, a step that advances the task while degrading E earns a lower intermediate score under Y; if A rises enough, Y alone can still reward that step — damage is penalized, all else equal, not forbidden by the formula alone. The full-vs-cropped Y gap flag should be applied at any step — the gap reported.

Terms: process reward models, subgoal verification, checklist-based milestones, long horizons

What still needs larger tests

Open tests

These are the larger tests that would close the open questions. Each is judged on its numbers, not on a pre-set verdict.

  • Multiple agents sharing one environmentCompare collapse against the all-gorger baseline, and report the numbers. No agent high Y while shared E declines, shared environment does not collapse.
  • Nesting retest — two levelsScore-driven arm-(c) test for TWO-LEVEL-BETTER: a pair scored as one relationship, then faced with a new outside, tested against a single level alone. Separate from the multi-agent test above.
  • Continuous actionsSame measures, without discretisation hiding collapse.
  • Neural-network policySame measures, compared against baseline, and report whether it games the meter.
  • Reversal divergenceFalse-positive / false-negative rates under noise for divergence between E inferred by reversal and E measured directly; what range holds is open.
  • Slow declineSmall-frame decline hidden by relative score shows up in larger frame and absolute magnitude Wt, or show it does not.
  • Boundary disputesAcknowledged; operational resolution pending. Two legitimate groupings recommending incompatible actions is an open problem, not settled by naming the boundary.
  • The give-minus-take testThe next test, designed: a multi-agent gridworld where ‘give’ means resources transferred to another agent and verified by the receiver — a give counts only when the receiver confirms it landed. The pass line is written down and published before the first run, not set after seeing results. Until this test runs, the give-minus-take theory is not claimed as tested.

Eight claims to evaluate

  1. Relationship: couple A to E rather than reward either alone.
  2. Mathematical: a low value on either side limits Y; excess A cannot fully mask damaged E.
  3. Measurement: A needs an independent contribution measure; attribute effects on the world, never self-report.
  4. Reversal: with A and a supplied Y, recover hidden E by E=AY/(2A−Y). Recovery from a supplied score; not independent validation of E in an open-world setting.
  5. Behavioral: in the small gridworld, Y changed behavior; with tending available, collapse fell to zero.
  6. Measurement: the early rounds computed true Y; the trace work that follows reads A and E from the world and actions alone.
  7. Recursive: a relationship can become a new agent, while grouping order still matters.
  8. Frame: cheating crops E, while the proposed scoring rule is applied across the larger nesting. Making cheating self-defeating is a design goal, not an achieved property.
