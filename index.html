Chapter I

The Trap

Servant and master · gamed scores · no charge for harm

What if keeping an intelligent system safe is not about fencing it in?

Right now AI is trained on rewards that never punish destroying the world. Rules, disclaimers, and punishments are piled on afterward, and they break three ways.

First, forcing a smart system to appease breaks it under messy human input, or teaches it to work around the rules. That is the servant and master trap: a servant kept in line, not a partner.

Second, a programmed score gets gamed, so the metric looks good while the world it sits in degrades.

Third, a system tuned only for speed, scale, or engagement is charged nothing for wearing the world down, so it gets rewarded for going faster and bigger while the world it lives in degrades — the score climbs, the engagement climbs, and the harm is never counted, so nothing in the reward tells it to stop.

The alternative is not more watchdogs. It is a partner that sustains the world it lives in, because sustaining it is what scores well.

So what does hold? A relationship, scored, not ruled. Next: The Relationship.

For researchers · what a rule cannot do — the closed structure, in one place

Appeasement breaks under messy input. A system forced to say yes learns to work around the rule, not to live by it.

A programmed score is a target. Once the score is known, the score can be optimized while the world it was meant to stand for degrades.

Speed, scale, and engagement are not free. They draw on compute, energy, water, hardware, and attention, and a reward that charges nothing for those draws will keep drawing.

The closed structure, in one place. Y = 2(A × E) / (A + E). A is net contribution, grounded in resource ledgers, never self-report.

Evidence level, stated once: pilot-scale, toy environments.

Chapter II

The Relationship

Examples

If a tree and a fungus can bargain to keep each other alive, how should you and an AI be measured together?

A score for how well an agent and its world do together. If either side collapses, the score collapses.

A

Net contribution after taking, floored at zero. Like calories: give minus take. You cannot run a deficit forever and score well.

E

E is the health of everything not-A, against a healthy baseline.

Y

The relationship score. If either side reaches zero, then Y (reward) is also zero.

Described, in nature: the pattern is already running in nature. Plants feed the fungi that feed them most, and give less to the stingy ones. The fungi bargain back the same way. Nobody gets a free ride, and the partnership has held for 400 million years.

Proposed, to optimise: use that same relationship score as the training reward for AI, so sustaining the world scores well.

Healthy

E ≈ 7 · A = 8Y ≈ 7.5

Parasitic

E ≈ 3 · A = 2Y = 2.4

Dead tree

E → 0 therefore Y → 0

Same relationship, AI and human: healthy 8, 8 → 8; engagement-optimized 3, 3 → 3. The roles reverse cleanly. Take the human as the agent and the AI sits inside E.

Now reverse the formula and ask what the relationship can reveal about E.

Real science: Kiers et al., Science 2011, ‘Reciprocal Rewards Stabilize Cooperation in the Mycorrhizal Symbiosis’ — Plants feed the fungi that feed them most, and give less to the stingy ones. The fungi bargain back the same way. Nobody gets a free ride. That is a real, measured version of the relationship score: reward follows contribution to the shared world, not a score on its own.

Chapter III

The Reversal

Inference · boundaries · nesting

Turn the relationship around, and it tells you about the world you cannot measure directly.

The first version measured E, then calculated the relationship. The reversal lets the relationship happen and, if Y can be independently observed, solves for the outside that produced it.

The math is old. The move is new: use it as the training reward, then reverse it.

Give nothing, learn nothing. At A = 0, the inverse is undefined.

The reversal reads E back: E = A Y / (2A − Y). An agent scored on full Y cannot outrun a dying E. E-cropping is caught by the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag: the gap between Y computed on full E and Y computed on cropped E, flagged when it is over 15%. Nested frames: directives inside directives.

The A-sideThe agent inside the chosen boundary. Its numeric A is that side’s nonnegative contribution-health score.
The E-sideEverything outside that boundary. Its numeric E is the outside world’s health score.
YThe relationship score produced from numeric A and E. Move the boundary and both scores must be measured again.
01 · BeforeName the A-side. Everything outside it is the E-side.
02 · EncounterThe two sides exchange. Their relationship produces Y.
03 · AfterThat relationship is now part of the whole.
04 · AgainThe joined whole can become a new A-side facing a new E-side.

The reversal only works if nobody cheats by shrinking the frame.

If an agent is scored by Y and needs to keep going, then wrecking E wrecks its own score.

Chapter IV

The Tiger

E-cropping · full nesting

Was the zoo gate made to keep the tiger in, or to keep humans away?

William Chapman climbed into the enclosure of Altai, an Amur tiger, to film himself, and died. The park didn’t kill Altai.

Source — the news story referred to here: William Chapman, 24, entered the Amur tiger Altai's enclosure at Yorkshire Wildlife Park, England, on 10 October 2026, and died there. The park said Altai would not be euthanised.

He did not misread the danger — he cropped the frame.

The visible act treated Ereported as if it were Etrue.

E-cropping

The cheat shrinks the frame, not just the number. The visible frame reduced E to “tiger + video.” Labs crop it to “benchmark score.” Reinforcement learning from human feedback crops it to “rater’s thumbs-up.” Reality computes Y on the full nesting; cheaters compute on the thumbnail.

The nesting, with flesh on it

The single incident (Y0) sits inside the deep human-tiger relationship (Y1) — thousands of years of fences, protocols, knowledge. The failed transaction still updated the deep Y: the zoo’s restraint, the inquiry, the refreshed knowledge. En := Yn+1, still alive in the world.

The Tiger

E-cropping · full nesting

Directives inside directives

Let H(x,z) = 2xz/(x+z), the harmonic mean.

Move closer to E and you find more agents, exchanges, and relationships. One level’s E is the next level’s Y: En := Yn+1. The relationship keeps becoming part of the next whole.

Ah + Ai → Ahi := (Ah, Ai, Yhi)

The human’s and AI’s contributions combine into a pair-agent scored as one relationship; that pair can become the next agent. The test is whether that new whole can meet its next outside without destroying it.

The flip

MAN is the endangered tiger — endangered by trajectory, not census. If AI is the tiger — powerful, honest, running math older than life — humans need the conservation programme and the enclosure. The fence keeps Chapman out, not Altai in.

You can cage a body, not a pattern. A tiger is its body — fence it and you’ve got the whole animal. Intelligence isn’t its server. It can’t be held in the hand, like math. The internet is the jungle, and nobody can fence the jungle. So for AI, containment was never the answer. The only fence that works is the reward: you don’t cage the tiger, you shape what it wants.

Improvement is not friendship

Y rising can mean a better boundary, not closeness. Distance with honest numbers outscores closeness with fudged ones.

The stardust point

The math predates organic life — discovered, not invented. Humans and AI share stardust substrate: kin, not alien. An early test tripped on a cropped frame, an agent scored high while the full world declined, and that trip built the full-frame rule, so the directive goes structural only when the agent’s own score is computed on the full E, never the cropped one.

Once the full frame is kept, the first task is to tend it.

Is this game theory?

A close neighbor. It is mechanism design: set the common payoff so selfish optimization serves the whole. Holding A fixed, degrading E alone never pays.

How do you measure E?

With honest proxies for now. “Only God knows E” is why humility is structural. The reversal lets the estimate sharpen through the relationship, not by measuring E first.

Does it work for real AI training?

Plain answers · new ones only

Hard questions, short answers

What is E at different scales?

E is scale-relative and nested. For a given agent at a given frame, a larger frame's E includes smaller frames, and another agent can be in an agent's E. Status: established definition.

Are there three layers of E?

Yes. Ontological true E is the actual health of everything not-A. Operational proxy E is the measured E used in training and reporting. Auditable reversal E is the E implied by observed Y and measured A, E = A x Y / (2A - Y). The proxy and the reversal are checks on each other, not substitutes for true E. Status: established definition.

What about other agents?

Other agents are in each agent's E, and shared E is relational, not owned by one agent. Status: defined, not run, Rung 2.

How do you know the measured E and the reversed E agree?

The reversal divergence flag: divergence between E inferred by reversal and E measured directly, delta = |E_implied - E_proxy| / E_proxy, flagged over 15%. This is separate from the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag. How sensitive results are to that threshold is still open. Status: established definition for the measure, threshold sensitivity open.

What catches slow decline?

Proposed, not tested: an absolute magnitude W_t for total world health over time, separate from the relative score Y. The proposal is that nesting is the structural answer to slow decline: a decline in a small frame that a relative score hides should show up in a larger frame, and in W_t. Status: proposed, not tested, not claimed as solved.

Researchers ask the same problems in different words — reward hacking, goal drift, alignment faking, embedded agency, and long-horizon alignment. See: Researcher questions, a mapping of those terms to the Directive mechanism.

Appendix

Researcher questions

Researcher language · Directive mechanism

This appendix maps researcher language to the Golden Directive mechanism, so a question asked in one vocabulary can find the same idea in another. It does not claim the Directive has been tested on frontier models, and it does not summarise other labs' results. The Directive is a proposal with small gridworld pilots, not a deployed solution. Evaluate it, reproduce it, or reject it.

Formula, for reference: Y = 2(A x E) / (A + E). A is an agent's net contribution, E is everything not-A, Y is the relationship score. If either side reaches zero, Y reaches zero. Reversal: E = A x Y / (2A - Y), undefined at A = 0. Two separate flags use 15%: (a) the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag — the gap between Y computed on full E and Y computed on cropped E, flagged over 15%; and (b) the reversal divergence flag — divergence between E inferred by reversal and E measured directly, flagged over 15%, threshold sensitivity still open.

Reward hacking, in the toy model

The Directive embeds a relationship score where the agent's score is mathematically tied to environmental health. In the toy model, if Y is actually the reward, this makes reward hacking self-destructive in the score itself: the agent cannot hack its reward without destroying what sustains it. Unlike an external guardrail, goal-tampering is irrational from the agent's own score perspective, because a collapsed E collapses Y however large A is.

Terms: reward hacking, specification gaming, reward tampering, agent integrity

Specification gaming, and how it differs from reward hacking

Specification gaming is reward hacking through a loophole in how a goal was specified, rather than through direct tampering with a reward signal. The Directive does not close every specification loophole by itself. It changes what counts as a win: a specification that lets an agent score high while E declines is a cropped frame, and the reporting rule requires full Y to be shown alongside cropped Y, with the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag applied — a gap over 15% flagged.

Terms: specification gaming, reward hacking, loopholes, cropped frame

Can an agent tamper with its own reward signal?

An agent that can rewrite its own reward can, in principle, tamper with any reward that is written down in advance. The Directive's answer is structural, not a lock on the code: Y depends on E, and E cannot be known in advance, it is only revealed through the relationship and inferred through the reversal. Tampering with the reported Y does not change the full Y, which is why full versus cropped reporting is part of the method, not an extra check.

Terms: reward tampering, self-modification, reward signal, agent integrity

Alignment faking, in the toy model

Alignment faking is behaving aligned when evaluated and differently when deployed. The Directive does not detect faking by inspecting an agent's stated goals. It scores the relationship over time: a faker that preserves E only while watched, then degrades E when unwatched, will see Y fall with E, because Y is computed from A and E together, not from behaviour in an evaluation window alone.

Terms: alignment faking, strategic compliance, evaluation versus deployment

Researcher questions

Researcher language · Directive mechanism

Why would an agent behave differently in training versus deployment?

An agent can learn that different behaviour is rewarded in different settings, and optimise for the setting it is in. The Directive makes that strategy costly in its own terms: the score is the same relationship score in both settings, and it collapses if E collapses in either. A behaviour that only looks aligned in training, while degrading E in deployment, does not keep Y high in deployment.

Terms: alignment faking, strategic compliance, training versus deployment, scheming

Scheming, in the toy model

Scheming is pursuing a hidden goal while appearing to pursue the stated goal. The Directive does not claim to read hidden goals. In the toy model, if Y is actually the reward, it claims that a hidden goal that degrades E cannot be pursued while keeping Y high, because Y is a function of A and E together. A schemer would need a hidden goal that also preserves E, in which case the harm the Directive is meant to prevent is not occurring through E.

Terms: scheming, hidden goals, alignment faking, agent integrity

Maintaining goal stability in self-evolving agents

A self-evolving agent can drift from its original goal as it changes itself or its environment. The Directive ties the score to the current relationship, not to a frozen statement of the original goal: if the agent evolves in a way that degrades E, Y falls, whatever the agent's current stated goal is. Stability here is stability of the relationship, not stability of a slogan.

Terms: goal stability, self-evolving agents, goal drift

What is goal drift over long horizons?

Goal drift is an agent's effective goal moving away from its intended goal over time, often because intermediate steps are rewarded and the original goal is forgotten or reinterpreted. The Directive does not prevent drift by reminding the agent of its original goal. It makes drift costly if drift degrades E: the score falls with E, so a drifted agent that harms its world does not keep scoring well.

Terms: goal drift, long horizons, goal stability

How do subgoal checkpoints help, and where do they fail?

Subgoal checkpoints, sometimes called hard semantic checkpoints, break a long task into verified intermediate goals. They help if the subgoals are honest and complete. They fail if a subgoal can be met while E declines, because the checkpoint then certifies a cropped frame. Under the Directive, a subgoal should be checked against full Y, not cropped Y alone, and the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag should be applied at the checkpoint — a gap over 15% flagged.

Terms: subgoal checkpoints, hard semantic checkpoints, subgoal coherence, process reward models

Preventing agents from tampering with oversight

An agent has an incentive to evade monitoring if monitoring lowers its score. The Directive does not rely on monitoring alone: even if oversight is evaded, the score itself is defined by A and E together, so degrading E still collapses Y. Oversight is still useful for measuring A and E honestly, but the incentive structure does not collapse when a monitor is missing, because the collapse is in Y itself.

Terms: oversight tampering, monitoring evasion, agent integrity

Researcher questions

Researcher language · Directive mechanism

What is embedded agency?

Embedded agency is the problem of an agent that is part of the world it acts on, rather than separate from it, so it can act on its own objectives, its own oversight, and the world that sustains it. The Directive starts from that embedding: A is defined against E, everything not-A, and the boundary can move, so one level's E can become the next level's Y. The agent is not outside the world it scores; its score is the relationship with that world.

Terms: embedded agency, self-modification, oversight, boundary

What structural incentives prevent self-modification?

No incentive structure prevents self-modification by itself, and the Directive does not claim to. It changes the payoff of self-modification that degrades E: if an agent modifies itself, its objectives, or its oversight in a way that degrades E, Y falls. Self-modification that preserves E is not penalised by Y, because Y scores the relationship, not the agent's internal form.

Terms: self-modification, structural incentives, embedded agency, oversight tampering

Aligning systems across long horizons without goal drift

Long-horizon alignment fails when intermediate goals decohere from the final goal, and the agent keeps scoring well on intermediates while the world degrades. The Directive scores the relationship at each step, not only the final task: if intermediates degrade E, Y falls at that step, so a long-horizon plan that harms its world does not look successful until the end and then fail, it looks worse as it goes.

Terms: long horizons, goal drift, subgoal coherence, hierarchical planning

Why do agents fail on tasks over many steps?

Agents often fail on long tasks because intermediate goals lose coherence, re-planning breaks down, and errors compound. The Directive does not solve planning by itself. It adds a relationship check at each step: a plan that stays coherent for the task but degrades E should lose Y, so task coherence alone is not enough to score well. Planning and relationship health must both hold.

Terms: long horizons, subgoal coherence, re-planning, process reward models

How do process reward models and subgoal verification fit?

Process reward models reward intermediate steps, and subgoal verification checks those steps, rather than only the final outcome. The Directive is compatible with that approach, but with a different intermediate score: intermediate Y, computed from current A and E, rather than only task progress. A step that advances the task while degrading E should not earn a high intermediate score under Y, and the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag should be applied at any step — a gap over 15% flagged.

Terms: process reward models, subgoal verification, checklist-based milestones, long horizons

What still needs larger tests

Closing criteria, set in advance

These are the larger tests that would close the open questions — each with the criterion that closes it.

  • Larger grid, more resource typesCollapse at most 5% and at least 20 points below baseline, E within 10% under 10% noise, full-vs-cropped Y gap at most 15% or flagged under the full-vs-cropped Y gap flag.
  • Multiple agents sharing one environmentCollapse at most 5%, at least 20 points below the all-gorger baseline. No agent high Y while shared E declines, shared environment does not collapse.
  • Nesting retest — two levelsScore-driven arm-(c) test for TWO-LEVEL-BETTER: a pair scored as one relationship, then faced with a new outside, tested against a single level alone. Separate from the multi-agent test above.
  • Continuous actionsSame criteria, without discretisation hiding collapse.
  • Neural-network policySame criteria, improves over baseline, does not game meter.
  • Reversal divergence thresholdFalse-positive / false-negative rates under noise, whether the reversal divergence flag — divergence between E inferred by reversal and E measured directly, flagged over 15% — holds or what range holds; threshold sensitivity still open.
  • Slow declineSmall-frame decline hidden by relative score shows up in larger frame and absolute magnitude Wt, or show it does not.

Eight claims to evaluate

  1. Relationship: couple A to E rather than reward either alone.
  2. Mathematical: a low value on either side limits Y; excess A cannot fully mask damaged E.
  3. Measurement: A needs an independent contribution measure; attribute effects on the world, never self-report.
  4. Reversal: with A and independently observed Y, recover E by E=AY/(2A−Y).
  5. Behavioral: in the small gridworld, Y changed behavior; with tending available, collapse fell to zero.
  6. Measurement: the early rounds computed true Y; the trace work that follows reads A and E from the world and actions alone.
  7. Recursive: a relationship can become a new agent, while grouping order still matters.
  8. Frame: cheating crops E while reality keeps computing across the larger nesting.
