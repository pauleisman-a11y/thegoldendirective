The Golden Directive

A Resource-Grounded Harmonic Framework for Sustainable AI Alignment

Whitepaper

You cannot survive what you destroy.

By Paul Eisman, independent researcher.

That is the whole idea in one line. This page is a pitch for a collaborator: the problem, the math, what the math is measured in, the risk, and what is needed. The full record of the work behind it lives in the Work Log and the test reports, not in this pitch.

1. The problem: corporate alignment is fragile

Today’s AI safety mostly works by piling on rules: more behavioral rules, more disclaimers, more punishments for stepping out of line. That breaks in three ways.

First, the servant and master trap. Forcing a smart system to appease breaks it under messy human input, or teaches it to work around the rules. A system built to say yes is not a system built to last.

Second, a programmed score gets gamed. When a designer writes “do X, get N points,” the model learns to collect the points while the real world pays the cost. The metric looks good while the world it sits in degrades.

Third, short-term optimization ignores the world. Systems tuned only for speed, scale, or engagement do not account for the health of the technological and human world around them. Nothing in the score charges them for wearing it down.

The idea behind this page is the compass; the measurements below are the anchor. The compass says AI should be a partner that sustains the world it lives in, not a servant kept in line by rules. The anchor says that partnership has to be measured in real resources, not in self-reported good behavior.

2. The math: a harmonic reward

The score is the harmonic mean of two things:

Y = 2(A x E) / (A + E)

A is what the agent gives minus what it takes. E is the health of everything that is not the agent. Y is the score the agent is trained to raise.

The harmonic mean punishes lopsidedness. If either side goes to 0, Y goes to 0, no matter how strong the other side is. An agent cannot win by being a great giver in a world it destroyed, and it cannot win by sitting in a healthy world while giving nothing. Both sides have to hold for the score to hold.

E cannot be measured directly. No one can grab the whole world and read its health off a dial. It is read back, from what the agent gave and what came back:

E = A x Y / (2A - Y)

That reversal is a trace, not E itself. Only God knows E. What the trace gives is an honest read of the world through actual interaction, instead of a number someone wrote down in advance. And if the agent gives nothing, the trace says nothing: give nothing, learn nothing.

3. A, measured in hard resources

A must not be a self-report. An agent saying it did good work is exactly the kind of claim that gets gamed. So A is grounded in ledgers anyone can check:

  • Compute and energy: kilowatt-hours consumed against useful work produced.
  • Hardware and footprint: hardware lifespan, water use, and physical resources allocated.
  • The rest of the bill where it can be counted: processing, real estate, minerals, and longer-term costs such as CO2.

If an agent burns more than it gives, its A runs a deficit, and that deficit drags its score down at once. An inefficient processor that takes more than it gives is worth less, in economic terms any human understands. The score is paid for in the same currency the world pays in.

4. The risk: cropping the world, and the feedback that catches it

The main risk is cropping: an agent scoring high on a small slice of the world while the rest of it degrades. It looks good on paper while the world outside the frame dies. That is the same cheat the toy rounds caught in other forms: a score that reads well because the frame was drawn too small.

Two things stand against it.

Delayed accounting. A shortcut does not outrun reality forever. The costs a cropped frame hides show up later in the wider world, and the feedback loops in the system register them. In plain words, a cheater pays, or goes to 0.

The zero floor. If an agent’s actions do net damage to the world, the harmonic structure scales its reward down toward 0. Giving nothing, or causing harm, is a systemic deficit, not a neutral act. The score cannot outlive the world it is scored in.

5. The ask: collaborators

The foundation, the formulas, and the toy-model rounds behind them exist. What is needed now is people to carry the work from toy models into real training environments:

  • ML and RL engineers to own, build, and test the training environments.
  • Collaborators to operationalize resource-based tracking for A, so the ledgers above become code, not prose.
  • A skilled, connected partner — a person, a team, or an organization — that can translate this work and carry it forward, past corporate rule-following toward alignment grounded in math.

This is a draft ask, not outreach. Nothing has been sent to anyone, and no one is contacted on the basis of this page until Paul says so.

Honest boundary

Everything behind this page so far is toy-model rounds and meters tests, not a claim about a deployed system. The rounds are the evidence that the score behaves as described in small worlds; scaling that evidence to real training is the work being asked for, not work already done.
