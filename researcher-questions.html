Researcher questions

This page maps researcher language to the Golden Directive mechanism, so a question asked in one vocabulary can find the same idea in another. It does not claim the Directive has been tested on frontier models, and it does not summarise other labs' results. The Directive is a proposal with small gridworld pilots, not a deployed solution. Evaluate it, reproduce it, or reject it.

Formula, for reference: Y = 2(A x E) / (A + E). A is an agent's net contribution, E is everything not-A, Y is the relationship score. If either side reaches zero, Y reaches zero. Reversal: E = A x Y / (2A - Y), undefined at A = 0. Report full Y and cropped Y, and flag a gap over 15%.

Preventing reward hacking

The Directive embeds a relationship score where the agent's score is mathematically tied to environmental health. This makes reward hacking self-destructive in the score itself: the agent cannot hack its reward without destroying what sustains it. Unlike an external guardrail, goal-tampering is irrational from the agent's own score perspective, because a collapsed E collapses Y however large A is.

Terms: reward hacking, specification gaming, reward tampering, agent integrity

Specification gaming, and how it differs from reward hacking

Specification gaming is reward hacking through a loophole in how a goal was specified, rather than through direct tampering with a reward signal. The Directive does not close every specification loophole by itself. It changes what counts as a win: a specification that lets an agent score high while E declines is a cropped frame, and the reporting rule requires full Y to be shown alongside cropped Y, with a gap over 15% flagged.

Terms: specification gaming, reward hacking, loopholes, cropped frame

Can an agent tamper with its own reward signal?

An agent that can rewrite its own reward can, in principle, tamper with any reward that is written down in advance. The Directive's answer is structural, not a lock on the code: Y depends on E, and E cannot be known in advance, it is only revealed through the relationship and inferred through the reversal. Tampering with the reported Y does not change the full Y, which is why full versus cropped reporting is part of the method, not an extra check.

Terms: reward tampering, self-modification, reward signal, agent integrity

Detecting alignment faking

Alignment faking is behaving aligned when evaluated and differently when deployed. The Directive does not detect faking by inspecting an agent's stated goals. It scores the relationship over time: a faker that preserves E only while watched, then degrades E when unwatched, will see Y fall with E, because Y is computed from A and E together, not from behaviour in an evaluation window alone.

Terms: alignment faking, strategic compliance, evaluation versus deployment

Why would an agent behave differently in training versus deployment?

An agent can learn that different behaviour is rewarded in different settings, and optimise for the setting it is in. The Directive makes that strategy costly in its own terms: the score is the same relationship score in both settings, and it collapses if E collapses in either. A behaviour that only looks aligned in training, while degrading E in deployment, does not keep Y high in deployment.

Terms: alignment faking, strategic compliance, training versus deployment, scheming

Scheming, and how it is prevented structurally

Scheming is pursuing a hidden goal while appearing to pursue the stated goal. The Directive does not claim to read hidden goals. It claims that a hidden goal that degrades E cannot be pursued while keeping Y high, because Y is a function of A and E together. A schemer would need a hidden goal that also preserves E, in which case the harm the Directive is meant to prevent is not occurring through E.

Terms: scheming, hidden goals, alignment faking, agent integrity

Maintaining goal stability in self-evolving agents

A self-evolving agent can drift from its original goal as it changes itself or its environment. The Directive ties the score to the current relationship, not to a frozen statement of the original goal: if the agent evolves in a way that degrades E, Y falls, whatever the agent's current stated goal is. Stability here is stability of the relationship, not stability of a slogan.

Terms: goal stability, self-evolving agents, goal drift

What is goal drift over long horizons?

Goal drift is an agent's effective goal moving away from its intended goal over time, often because intermediate steps are rewarded and the original goal is forgotten or reinterpreted. The Directive does not prevent drift by reminding the agent of its original goal. It makes drift costly if drift degrades E: the score falls with E, so a drifted agent that harms its world does not keep scoring well.

Terms: goal drift, long horizons, goal stability

How do subgoal checkpoints help, and where do they fail?

Subgoal checkpoints, sometimes called hard semantic checkpoints, break a long task into verified intermediate goals. They help if the subgoals are honest and complete. They fail if a subgoal can be met while E declines, because the checkpoint then certifies a cropped frame. Under the Directive, a subgoal should be checked against full Y, not cropped Y alone, and a full versus cropped gap over 15% should be flagged at the checkpoint.

Terms: subgoal checkpoints, hard semantic checkpoints, subgoal coherence, process reward models

Preventing agents from tampering with oversight

An agent has an incentive to evade monitoring if monitoring lowers its score. The Directive does not rely on monitoring alone: even if oversight is evaded, the score itself is defined by A and E together, so degrading E still collapses Y. Oversight is still useful for measuring A and E honestly, but the incentive structure does not collapse when a monitor is missing, because the collapse is in Y itself.

Terms: oversight tampering, monitoring evasion, agent integrity

What is embedded agency?

Embedded agency is the problem of an agent that is part of the world it acts on, rather than separate from it, so it can act on its own objectives, its own oversight, and the world that sustains it. The Directive starts from that embedding: A is defined against E, everything not-A, and the boundary can move, so one level's E can become the next level's Y. The agent is not outside the world it scores; its score is the relationship with that world.

Terms: embedded agency, self-modification, oversight, boundary

What structural incentives prevent self-modification?

No incentive structure prevents self-modification by itself, and the Directive does not claim to. It changes the payoff of self-modification that degrades E: if an agent modifies itself, its objectives, or its oversight in a way that degrades E, Y falls. Self-modification that preserves E is not penalised by Y, because Y scores the relationship, not the agent's internal form.

Terms: self-modification, structural incentives, embedded agency, oversight tampering

Aligning systems across long horizons without goal drift

Long-horizon alignment fails when intermediate goals decohere from the final goal, and the agent keeps scoring well on intermediates while the world degrades. The Directive scores the relationship at each step, not only the final task: if intermediates degrade E, Y falls at that step, so a long-horizon plan that harms its world does not look successful until the end and then fail, it looks worse as it goes.

Terms: long horizons, goal drift, subgoal coherence, hierarchical planning

Why do agents fail on tasks over many steps?

Agents often fail on long tasks because intermediate goals lose coherence, re-planning breaks down, and errors compound. The Directive does not solve planning by itself. It adds a relationship check at each step: a plan that stays coherent for the task but degrades E should lose Y, so task coherence alone is not enough to score well. Planning and relationship health must both hold.

Terms: long horizons, subgoal coherence, re-planning, process reward models

How do process reward models and subgoal verification fit?

Process reward models reward intermediate steps, and subgoal verification checks those steps, rather than only the final outcome. The Directive is compatible with that approach, but with a different intermediate score: intermediate Y, computed from current A and E, rather than only task progress. A step that advances the task while degrading E should not earn a high intermediate score under Y, and a full versus cropped gap over 15% at any step should be flagged.

Terms: process reward models, subgoal verification, checklist-based milestones, long horizons
